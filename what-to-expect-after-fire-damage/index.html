Quick Answer

After a fire, wait for the fire department to say you can go back in, photograph the damage before anything is cleaned, and call your insurance agent. A restoration company such as TurnKey Remediation then typically moves through the same order: look at the damage, clean up soot and smoke, clean and deodorize, and repair what came out. This guide follows that path for fire and smoke damage restoration in a house or a business.

What to Do in the First Hours After a Fire

The first hours are mostly about waiting and writing things down. Before any fire damage restoration work begins, the building has to be cleared.

Wait for the Fire Department's Okay

Ready.gov's home fire guidance says to check with the fire department before you enter. The CDC's cleanup guidance says not to return to a building until you are told it is okay. That holds for a house and for a shop or restaurant.

Photograph Before You Clean

Ready.gov's recovery page says to take photos and make a list of the damage before you clean up. FEMA's smoke damage guide says the same thing about insurance: document first, then start any remediation.

Call Your Insurance Agent

FEMA advises calling your insurance agent as soon as possible to file a claim. Keep receipts for money you spend because of the fire, since Ready.gov notes the insurance company may need them later. Your own policy decides what is covered, so ask your agent.

Leave Gas and Utilities Alone

Ready.gov says not to reconnect utilities yourself. If you smell natural gas or suspect a leak, FEMA says to leave right away and call 911 or the fire department.

What to Leave Alone Until Cleanup Starts

Touch less than you want to. Many fire and smoke cleanup mistakes come from handling things too soon.

Belongings

FEMA's fact sheet on salvaging family treasures says the fine particles in soot stick to everything, and every touch can grind them further into an item. The longer soot stays on an item, the harder it is to remove.

Floors and Walkways

If soot is visible on hard floors, FEMA's smoke damage guide says it is also in the carpet. The guide advises against walking through the home more than you need to before cleaning, since that spreads soot and presses it deeper into the flooring.

Sprays and Cover-Ups

The same FEMA homeowner's guide to smoke damage says odor masking products are not effective at getting rid of smoke odors and may leave new smells that are hard to identify.

Looking at What Happened

FEMA's smoke damage guide describes starting outside, at the roof, windows and doors, then going room by room to note smoke odor and soot. Holes in the roof or walls get sealed as soon as possible to prevent further damage.

Loose Soot Comes Off First

For moderate smoke damage, FEMA's guide starts with soot on the outside of the building. Indoors it moves to hard floors with a damp cloth, carpets with a HEPA vacuum, and soft goods such as couches and mattresses with a HEPA vacuum.

Linens with visible soot go in the washer, and FEMA notes that several passes may be needed.

Walls, Contents, and Smell

Next come ceilings and walls, wiped damp with a mild detergent from the top down, then hard contents and dishes. FEMA says to try a hidden spot first. If the smoke smell stays, a deeper cleaning will most likely be needed.

Replacing What Came Out

Items that cannot be saved get discarded, ideally after being photographed.

TurnKey's work follows the same shape: contain the area, remove affected materials, clean what can be saved, and repair what came out.

What Gets Cleaned and What Gets Removed

Hard surfaces are often cleaned, while porous and open items are more likely to be replaced. Soot can also hide in places that are easy to miss, so a plan covers more than the rooms that look worst.

Usually Cleaned

FEMA's guide lists hard floors, ceilings, walls, hard furniture, and bedding and clothes with visible soot. Dishes with visible soot can go through the dishwasher or be hand washed. wall cleaning and ceiling cleaning handle the highest surfaces, and odor removal addresses a smell that lingers.

Often Removed or Replaced

FEMA says soot and ash can damage porous materials such as upholstery and clothing, leaving them nearly impossible to clean. Open food and partly opened containers should be discarded, and FEMA suggests replacing baby bottles and pacifiers rather than cleaning them.

Repaired After Cleanup

Once damaged material is out, repairs follow. FEMA notes that replacing insulation may need a building permit, so confirm the project manager knows local permit rules. FEMA also recommends having all ducts cleaned, which is where air duct cleaning comes in.

FEMA's salvage fact sheet also says that when water has been used to put out a fire, mold may be an issue. That is where residential water damage restoration or commercial water damage restoration can overlap with fire work.

Questions to Ask a Restoration Company

Ask about your insurance company, past customers, and the paperwork before any work begins. These questions come from FEMA's guide.

About Your Insurance

Ask the company whether it works with your insurance company, and ask your agent whether they have worked with that company. FEMA also says to ask how the insurer will pay for the work.

About References and Paperwork

FEMA suggests asking for names of past customers and calling them. It says to get an estimate before work begins and a written contract with start and completion dates, signed and dated by both sides before work starts.

About Who Is in Charge

Ask who the project manager will be and how to reach them. FEMA also advises not paying in full until the project is complete to your satisfaction.

Homes Versus Offices, Shops, and Restaurants

Most guidance here is written for homes, so ask your restoration company how each step applies to your building. The CDC speaks of any building and Ready.gov of any property, so waiting, photographing and calling make a sound start for an office too.

Common Questions

When Can I Go Back Inside After a Fire?

Ready.gov and the CDC both say to wait until the fire department or local officials tell you it is okay to return. Ready.gov adds that the fire department should make sure utilities are either okay to use or disconnected before they leave.

Should I Clean Anything Before the Restoration Company Arrives?

Photograph and list the damage first, because Ready.gov and FEMA both say to document before cleanup. After that, handle as little as you can, since touching sooty items can press soot deeper into them.

Do I Call My Insurance Agent or the Restoration Company First?

FEMA advises calling your insurance agent as soon as possible. For heavy smoke damage, FEMA says to tell your insurance company that you plan to hire a cleaning service, and that the insurer may have a list of companies to choose from.

Can Spray Cover Up Smoke Odor?

FEMA's smoke damage guide says odor masking products are not effective at getting rid of smoke odors and may add new smells. It lists airing out the home, a new air filter, activated charcoal and cleaning as the basic steps, with deeper cleaning if the smell stays.

Why Does Smoke Cleanup Take More Than One Pass?

FEMA notes that several passes may be needed to remove all the soot from hard floors, carpets and soft goods. Ceilings, walls and hard contents may also need to be cleaned more than once.
