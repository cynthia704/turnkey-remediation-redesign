Quick Answer

After a fire, smoke damage hides where soot settles and where porous materials soak up odor, including attics, walls, ducts, closets, and kitchens. A room can look clean while residue and smell remain in places you cannot see. This guide from TurnKey Remediation walks through each spot, in a house or a business, so you know where to look first.

Why Smoke Damage Is Easy to Miss

Smoke damage is easy to miss because smoke carries gases and tiny particles, and the two end up in different places. FEMA's homeowner's guide to residential smoke damage explains that the gases are absorbed into the surrounding air and porous materials, while the particles settle on surfaces as soot and ash.

That split explains a common surprise. A ceiling can look fine while a closet down the hall still smells like the fire.

What Smoke Leaves Behind

Smoke leaves three kinds of damage: soot on surfaces, odor in porous materials, and corrosion on metal. Each one hides in a different way.

Soot Residue

Soot is made of small, dark particles left by incomplete burning. The Wikipedia entry on soot notes that it settles onto surfaces, can darken them, and is the main cause of ghosting, the discoloration where walls meet ceilings or floors.

Soot is also oily. It stains fabric, and rubbing it or vacuuming it with a brush attachment can push it deeper.

Trapped Odor

Odor is the part you cannot wipe off. Smoke smell can stay in upholstered furniture, carpet, drapes, and clothing, and in porous materials such as wallboard and plaster.

Perfume sprays and other odor maskers only cover the smell. The smell can return once the spray fades.

Corrosion on Metal

Left on metal, residue can do lasting harm. FEMA's guide says fire residue that sits on a surface can permanently etch metal, and county cleanup guidance recommends treating metal finishes to prevent rust and staining.

Attic Insulation

Insulation may hold on to smoke odor, so it is one of the first things to ask about when the smell will not leave. The attic sits above the rooms people walk through, which makes it easy to skip. Ask for it to be looked at, and expect a conversation about replacing insulation that kept the smell.

Painted Walls and Plaster

Paint, wallboard, and plaster are porous, so soot and odor can sink in, and smoke can permeate walls. Faint ghosting where a wall meets the ceiling is an early sign. Start with a dry chemical sponge, because water-based cleaners can make a stain bleed into plaster.

Ducts and Vents

Smoke can drift through household ducts and become trapped in their joints and crevices, which are hard to clean with ordinary tools. You cannot see inside, so a smell can come back after the rooms are clean. Wait to run the air handler until most of the soot is gone, because running it can spread the particles.

Kitchen Cabinets and Drawers

After a kitchen fire, residue may not be readily visible, and cabinets and drawer contents may have to come out to reach the hidden areas. Closed doors keep the damage out of sight. Open everything, photograph what you find, and keep a list of what was inside.

Closets and Stored Fabrics

Clothes, bedding, and linens can hold smoke odor for a very long time, even inside a shut closet. Nobody sorts through a closet while the main rooms need attention. Wash items by their care labels, expect more than one round, and check the closet walls and shelves too.

Hidden Smoke Damage in a Shop, Office, or Restaurant

A shop, office, or restaurant hides smoke damage in much the same places a house does, because the materials are the same. Carpet, upholstered chairs, and stored fabrics such as linens or uniforms can hold odor.

Painted walls and ceilings collect soot like any other porous surface, which is where wall cleaning, ceiling cleaning, and carpet cleaning come in.

A building's HVAC system can also spread soot through every room it serves if it runs too soon. For larger spaces, commercial cleaning covers the surfaces and the air handling side by side.

What to Do Before Anything Gets Cleaned

Before anything gets cleaned, get permission to go back in, document what you find, and leave the air handler off. Those three steps protect the property and your paperwork.

Wait for the Okay to Go Back In

Do not enter a fire-damaged building until the fire department or local authorities say you can. The American Red Cross also warns against crossing colored tape on doors or windows unless local authorities advise it.

Photograph Before You Move Things

Take photographs of the damage before you move or throw anything out. The Red Cross advises keeping damaged goods until an inventory has been taken.

Then check your own policy and call your insurance agent. Your policy decides what is covered, and your agent can explain the next step.

Hold Off on the Air Handler and the Sprays

FEMA's guide says to leave the HVAC air handler off until most of the soot has been removed, because running it spreads particles and dirties surfaces that were already cleaned. Napa County's fire cleanup page and FEMA both say odor-masking products only cover the problem.

Common Questions

Can Smoke Damage Be Hidden After a Fire?

Yes. Soot and odor can sit in ducts, closets, kitchen cabinets, and porous materials where nobody looks. After a kitchen fire in particular, residue may not be readily visible.

Why Does My House Still Smell Like Smoke After Cleaning?

Smoke gases are absorbed into porous materials, and odor can remain in upholstery, carpet, drapes, and clothing unless those items are properly deodorized. Smoke trapped in ducts can also bring the smell back.

Does Smoke Damage Reach Rooms Far From the Fire?

It can. Smoke and soot can travel into other rooms and affect paint, carpet, upholstery, drapes, clothing, and other belongings.

Should I Run the Air Conditioning After a Fire?

FEMA's guide says to leave the HVAC air handler off until the bulk of the soot and ash has been removed. Running it sooner can spread the particles and dirty surfaces that were already cleaned.

Can Smoke Damage Hurt Metal Surfaces?

It can. Fire residue left on a surface may permanently etch metal, and county cleanup guidance recommends treating metal finishes to prevent rust and staining.
