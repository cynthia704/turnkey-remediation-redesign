Fire and Smoke Cleanup Mistakes to Avoid

The common missteps after a fire at a house or a business, and what federal guidance says to do instead.

Quick Answer

Most fire and smoke cleanup mistakes come from rushing: going in too early, rubbing at soot, or throwing things out before they are photographed. TurnKey Remediation handles fire and smoke damage restoration for homes and businesses, and the guidance below comes from FEMA, the U.S. Fire Administration and other government sources.

Wait for the Fire Department Before Going Back In

Stay out until the fire department says you can enter. The U.S. Fire Administration's After the Fire guide says not to enter a damaged home or apartment unless the fire department clears it, and Ready.gov's home fire guidance says to check with them before you go in.

Utilities fall under the same rule. The U.S. Fire Administration says firefighters make sure water, electricity and gas are okay to use or have them turned off, and that you should not turn them back on yourself. Ready.gov says the same about reconnecting utilities.

FEMA's guide to salvaging belongings after a fire also tells people to check for structural damage before re-entering. A shop, office or restaurant owner can ask the fire department the same question about the building before staff or deliveries go in.

Once the building is cleared, what to expect after fire damage lays out the restoration process stage by stage, and fire restoration covers the repairs that follow.

Hands Off the Soot Until You Have a Plan

Soot can spread and set when it is touched, rubbed or walked through, so handle as little as you can. FEMA's guide for salvaging belongings says the very fine particles in soot stick to everything and every touch can grind them further in.

Rubbing and Brushing

For belongings, the Library of Congress advises blotting soot off instead of rubbing, and notes that hand oils can drive soot into items. FEMA suggests vacuuming soot off as soon as possible, ideally with a HEPA filter, without a brush and without letting the nozzle touch the surface.

Wet Cloths on Belongings

FEMA says water can drive soot and ash further into the surface of an item. Its guide also says not to vacuum items that are wet or damp, so they need to dry first.

Walking Through the House

FEMA's smoke-damage guide for homeowners says that if soot is visible on hard floors, it is also on the carpet. It advises against walking around the home more than needed before cleaning, because that can spread soot and press it further into the flooring.

Start High When You Wipe Walls and Ceilings

When you wipe walls and ceilings, work from the high surfaces down. That is the order in FEMA's smoke-damage guide for homeowners, written after the Marshall Fire.

Ceilings and Walls

The guide says to damp wipe ceilings and walls with water and a mild detergent or all-purpose cleaner, starting high and working downward. It also says to clean a hidden spot first to be sure the water or cleaner will not damage the surface, and that it may take more than one pass.

Floors and Soft Goods

The same guide has people clean hard floors with a damp cloth or cleaning pad and vacuum carpets with a HEPA vacuum, often over several passes. Bedding, towels and clothes with visible soot go in the washer, and couches and mattresses get the HEPA vacuum.

Smoke Odor and Hidden Soot

A smoke smell can linger after the surfaces look clean, and odor removal deals with it. Soot also settles where nobody looks, which is the subject of where smoke damage hides after a fire.

A restaurant kitchen or an office floor is a bigger version of the same job, and commercial cleaning covers it.

Keep the Air Handler Off Until the Soot Is Gone

Hold off on running the furnace or air conditioner fan until most of the soot is removed. FEMA's homeowner guide says running the air handler before the bulk of the soot is gone can spread it around and dirty surfaces that were already cleaned.

After most of the soot is out, the guide has people wipe the register covers and tape a lightweight fabric such as cheesecloth over each register before starting the unit. It also calls for a new air filter rated MERV 11 or higher and recommends having all ducts and the furnace cleaned by a cleaning service, which is where air duct and HVAC cleaning comes in.

Photograph First, Then Throw Things Out

Document damage before anything leaves the building. Ready.gov says not to throw away damaged goods until you have made an inventory of your items.

Take Photos and Make a List

FEMA's guide for salvaging belongings says to photograph damaged items for insurance purposes. The smoke-damage guide adds that anything you discard should be photographed first.

Save Your Receipts

The U.S. Fire Administration says to save receipts for money spent because of the fire, since the insurance company may need them later. Ready.gov says the same.

Call Your Insurance Agent Early

The U.S. Fire Administration says to contact your insurance company right away and ask what to do first, and FEMA says to call your agent as soon as possible. Check your own policy for what it covers, and a business owner can do the same with the business policy.

Be Careful With Food That Was Near the Fire

Food that sat near a fire is often not worth keeping. The U.S. Department of Agriculture says saving food that has been in a fire generally is not a good idea.

It says food in permeable packaging such as cardboard or plastic wrap should be thrown away. FEMA's smoke-damage guide likewise says open or partly opened containers, such as cereal boxes, should be discarded, with a photo of each item discarded.

Common Questions

When Can I Go Back Into My House After a Fire?

When the fire department says you can. The U.S. Fire Administration says not to enter a damaged home unless the fire department clears it, and Ready.gov says to check with them first.

Can I Wipe Soot Off With a Wet Cloth?

For belongings, FEMA says water can drive soot deeper into the surface, and it suggests vacuuming instead. For walls and ceilings, FEMA's homeowner guide does call for damp wiping with mild detergent, after a hidden spot has been tried first.

Should I Turn on the HVAC After a Fire?

FEMA's homeowner guide says to wait until most of the soot has been removed. Running the air handler sooner can spread soot and dirty surfaces that were already cleaned.

Should I Keep Food That Was Near the Fire?

The U.S. Department of Agriculture says saving food that has been in a fire generally is not a good idea. Food in cardboard, plastic wrap or open containers should be thrown away.

When Should I Call My Insurance Agent?

Right away, according to the U.S. Fire Administration, which also says to ask what to do first. FEMA likewise says to call as soon as possible.
